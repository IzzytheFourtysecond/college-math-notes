As a side note, the prior theorem can fail when we allow $M$ and $N$ to have nonempty\\ boundaries. For an obvious example of this, let $M = H^n$ and $N = \mathbb{R}^n$. Then let\\ $\iota: H^n \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ be the inclusion map and consider any $p \in \partial H^n$. We already know from earlier that $\df \iota_p$ is a bijection. That said, the prior theorem holding in this situation would\newpage contradict the invariance of domain theorem on \inLinkRap{Invariance of Domain Theorem}{page 1008}. So, no such open sets $U_0$\\ and $V_0$ can exist in this case.\retTwo

\Hstatement\blab{Exercise 4-2:} Suppose $M$ is a $C^r$ manifold (without a boundary) and $N$ is a $C^r$ manifold with a boundary. Also let $\Int(M)$ denote the set of interior / non-boundary points of $N$. Then if $F: N \to M$ is a $C^k$ map (where $k \leq r$), $p \in M$, and $\df F_p$ is bijective, we must have that $F(p) \in \Int(N)$.
\begin{myIndent}\HexOne
	Proof:\\
	Once again, the fact that $\df F_p$ is injective means that there exists an integer $n$ with\\ $\dim(M) = n = \dim(N)$. Now let $(U, \varphi)$ be a smooth chart on $M$ containing $p$ and $(V, \psi)$ be a smooth chart on $N$ with $F(U) \subseteq V$. Since $M$ has no boundary, we know that $\widehat{U} \coloneqq \varphi(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$.\retTwo

	Yet now note that $\widehat{F} \coloneqq \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is a $C^k$ map from $\widehat{U}$ into $\mathbb{R}$ such that $\Df \widehat{F}(\varphi(p))$ is invertible. It follows by the inverse function theorem in my math 140c notes that there are open sets $\widehat{U}_0$ and $\widehat{V}_0$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ such that $\varphi(p) \in \widehat{U}_0 \subseteq \widehat{U}$ and $\widehat{F}$ is a bijection from $\widehat{U}_0$ to $\widehat{V}_0$. So $(\psi|_{\psi^{-1}(\widehat{V}_0)}, \psi^{-1}(\widehat{V}_0))$ is an interior chart on $N$ containing $F(p)$. $\blacksquare$\retTwo
\end{myIndent}

\pracOne To start off, it's been bugging me for several days that I don't have a way of talking about tangent spaces to corner points of $C^r$ manifolds (where $r < \infty$). Let $M$ be a $C^r$ manifold with a corner point $p$. Then we define $\mathcalli{C}(M, p)$ and $\mathcalli{V}_p M$ as on \inLinkRap{Page 1046 reference}{page 1046}. Also, we let $\mathcalli{D}_p M$ denote the space of derivations at $p$ from $C^1(M, \mathbb{R})$ to $\mathbb{R}$. Now for any $\gamma \in \mathcalli{C}(M, p)$ we know $D_\gamma: C^1(M, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $D_\gamma(f) \coloneqq (f \circ \gamma)^\prime(0)$ is a well-defined derivation at $p$. Also, we know by the definition of the equivalence classes in $\mathcalli{V}_p(M)$ that $[\gamma_1] = [\gamma_2]$ in $\mathcalli{V}_p$ if and only if $D_{\gamma_1} = D_{\gamma_2}$.\retTwo
\begin{myIndent}
	\ul{Lemma:} Let $S \coloneqq \{D_\gamma : [\gamma] \in \mathcalli{V}_p M\}$. Then let $T_p M$ be the subspace of $\mathcalli{D}_p M$ spanned by $S$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item[(a)] If $M$ is an $n$-dimensional manifold then $T_p M$ is also $n$-dimensional.
		\begin{myIndent}\pracTwo
			Proof:\\
			Let $(U, \varphi)$ be a smooth chart on $M$ containing $p$, and then recall for any\\ $f \in C^1(M, \mathbb{R})$ and $[\gamma] \in \mathcalli{V}_p M$ that:

			{\centering $\Df_\gamma(f) = (f \circ \gamma)^\prime(0) = \nabla(f \circ \varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p)) \cdot (\varphi \circ \gamma)^\prime(0)$ \retTwo\par}

			Now suppose $\gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_{n+1}$ are all in $\mathcalli{C}(M, p)$. Then for any $c_1, \ldots, c_{n+1}$\\ we have that:

			{\centering $\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} c_i D_{\gamma_i}\right)(f) = \nabla(f \circ \varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p)) \cdot \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} c_i(\varphi \circ \gamma_i)^\prime(0)\right)$ \retTwo\par}

			Yet because $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an $n$-dimensional vector space, we know there exists\\ $c_1, \ldots, c_{n+1}$ which aren't all zero such that $\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} c_i(\varphi \circ \gamma_i)^\prime(0) = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$.\newpage And then we'll have $\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} c_i D_{\gamma_i}\right)(f) = \nabla(f \circ \varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p)) \cdot 0 = 0$ for all $f \in C^1(M, \mathbb{R})$. This proves that $\dim(T_p M) \leq n$.\retTwo

			As for the other inequality, note that because $\varphi(U)$ is open in $(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^n$, we can guarentee if $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ is the standard basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$ then there exists $R > 0$ with $\varphi(p) + te_i \in \varphi(U)$ for all $t \in [0, R]$ and each $i$. So, I define $\gamma_i: [0, R] \to M$ by $\gamma(t) \coloneqq \varphi^{-1}(\varphi(p) + te_i)$. Then for any $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ we have that:

			{\centering $\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i D_{\gamma_i}\right)(f) = \nabla(f \circ \varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p)) \cdot (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$ \retTwo\par}

			At last, we just need to show that if $\mVecAst{c} \coloneqq (c_1, \ldots, c_n) \neq 0$ then there exists some $f \in C^1(M, \mathbb{R})$ with $\nabla(f \circ \varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p)) \cdot \mVecAst{c} \neq 0$. Fortunately, we know from the proof of the lemma on \inLinkRap{Lemma page 1047}{page 1047} how to go a step further and find $f \in C^r(M, \mathbb{R})$ with $\nabla(f \circ \varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p)) \cdot \mVecAst{c} \neq 0$. Therefore:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item $D_{\gamma_1}, \ldots, D_{\gamma_n}$ is a collection of linearly independent vectors in $S \subseteq \mathcalli{D}_p M$.
				\item If we restrict the domains of all the derivations in $T_p M$ to just $C^r(M, \mathbb{R})$ then we still have that $D_{\gamma_1}, \ldots, D_{\gamma_n}$ are linearly independent vectors.\retTwo
			\end{itemize}
		\end{myIndent}

		\item[(b)] Let $F: M \to N$ be a $C^1$ map. Then let $S^\prime \coloneqq \{D_\gamma : [\gamma] \in \mathcalli{V}_{F(p)} N\}$ and let $T_{F(p)} N$ be the subspace of $\mathcalli{D}_{F(p)} N$ spanned by $S^\prime$.
		\begin{myIndent}\color{red}
			[Note that if $F(p)$ is not a corner point of $N$, then this definition agress with our definition from \inLinkRap{Page 1051 reference}{page 1051} because $T_{F(p)}N$ would equal $S^\prime$].\retTwo
		\end{myIndent}
		
		Now define $\df F_p : \mathcalli{D}_p M \to \mathcalli{D}_{F(p)}N$ by $\df F_p(v)f \coloneqq v(f \circ F)$. Then we have that $\df F_p(T_p M) \subseteq T_{F(p)}N$.
		\begin{myIndent}\pracTwo
			Proof:\\
			It suffices to show that $\df F_p(S) \subseteq S^\prime$. Fortunately, given any $D_\gamma \in S$ we have that $\df F_p (D_{\gamma})f = D_\gamma(f \circ F) = (f \circ F \circ \gamma)^\prime(0) = D_{F \circ \gamma} f$. Therefore\\ $\df F_p (D_{\gamma}) = D_{F \circ \gamma} \in S^\prime$. $\blacksquare$\retTwo
		\end{myIndent}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{myIndent}

Based on the prior lemma, we now have a way of defining $T_p M$ when $p$ is a corner point. Also by part (b), we know that the differential of $F: M \to N$ at $p$ gives a well defined linear map $T_p M \to T_{F(p)}N$.\retTwo

The proofs for propositions 3.6, 3.8, and 3.9 on pages \inLinkRap{Proposition 3.6 page 1031}{1031} and \inLinkRap{page 1033 reference}{1033-1034} can now be repeated verbatim for our modified definition of $T_p M$ for when $p$ is a corner chart and $M$ is a $C^r$ manifold with $r \neq \infty$ necessarily. Similarly, nothing on \inLinkRap{page 1051-1053 reference}{pages 1051-1053} breaks down with our definition for this special case either. Finally, proposition 4.1 on \inLinkRap{Proposition 4.1 pages 1054-1056}{pages 1054-1056} as exercise 4-2 on the prior page still work by identical reasoning.\retTwo

Then let $M \coloneqq M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k$ be their product\\ manifold, and for each $i$ let $\pi_i: M \to M_i$ be the projection map $(p_1, \ldots, p_k) \mapsto p_i$.\newpage
\begin{myIndent}\myComment
	Remark: Given any finite collection of $C^r$ manifolds $M_1, \ldots, M_k$ with corners, the standard product $C^r$-structure on $M \coloneqq M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k$ defined on \inLinkRap{proposition bottom of page 1038}{page 1038} is still well-defined. \end{myIndent}

\exTwo\ul{Proposition 3.14:} Let $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_k) \in M$. Then the map $\alpha: T_p M \to \bigoplus_{i=1}^k T_{p_i}M_i$ defined by $\alpha(v) \coloneqq (\df (\pi_1)_p (v), \ldots, \df (\pi_k)_p (v))$ is an isomorphism.
\begin{myIndent}\exThreeP
	Proof:\\
	For each $i$ let us define $\iota_i: M_i \to M$ by $\iota_i(q) \coloneqq (p_1, \ldots, p_{i-1}, q, p_{i+1}, \ldots, p_k)$. Then, we define $\beta: \bigoplus_{i=1}^k T_{p_i}M_i \to T_p M$ by $\beta(v_1, \ldots, v_k) \coloneqq \sum_{i=1}^k \df (\iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)$. I claim that\\ $\beta = \alpha^{-1}$. To see why, first note that:

	{\centering\begin{tabular}{l}
		$(\alpha \circ \beta)(v_1, \ldots, v_k) = \alpha\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k \df (\iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)\right)$\\ [12pt]
		$\phantom{(\alpha \circ \beta)(v_1, \ldots, v_k)} = \left(\df (\pi_1)_p\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k \df (\iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)\right), \ldots, \df (\pi_k)_p\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k \df (\iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)\right)\right)$\\ [16pt]
		$\phantom{(\alpha \circ \beta)(v_1, \ldots, v_k)} = \left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_1)_p(\df (\iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)), \ldots, \sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_k)_p(\df (\iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i))\right)$\\ [16pt]
		$\phantom{(\alpha \circ \beta)(v_1, \ldots, v_k)} = \left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_1 \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i), \ldots, \sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_k \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)\right)$
	\end{tabular}\retTwo\par}

	But now $\pi_j \circ \iota_i$ is a constant map if $j \neq i$. As a result, if $v \in T_{p_i}M_i$ and $f \in C^1(M_j, \mathbb{R})$ then $f \circ \pi_j \circ \iota_i$ is a constant function from $M_i$ to $\mathbb{R}$ and in turn:
	
	{\centering$\df (\pi_j \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)f = v_i(f \circ \pi_j \circ \iota_i) = 0$.\retTwo\par}
	
	Since $f$ was arbitrary, this shows that if $j \neq i$ then $\df (\pi_j \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v)$ is the zero map. And since $v$ was arbitary, we can conclude $\df (\pi_j \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}$ is also the zero map. Meanwhile, $\pi_i \circ \iota_i = \myId_{M_i}$. Hence, we can conclude that:

	{\centering\begin{tabular}{l}
		$\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_1 \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i), \ldots, \sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_k \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)\right) = (\df (\myId_{M_1})_{p_1}(v_1), \ldots, \df (\myId_{M_k})_{p_k}(v_k))$\\ [6pt]
		$\phantom{\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_1 \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i), \ldots, \sum\limits_{i=1}^k\df (\pi_k \circ \iota_i)_{p_i}(v_i)\right)} = (v_1, \ldots, v_k)$.
	\end{tabular}\retTwo\par}

	With that, we've proven that $\beta$ is a right inverse of $\alpha$. Based on the fact that $\alpha$ has a right inverse, we know that $\alpha$ is a surjective linear map. And since $T_p M$ and $\bigoplus_{i=1}^k T_{p_i}M_i$ are both $(n_1 + \ldots + n_k)$-dimensional vector spaces and $\alpha$ is a linear map, we can conclude more strongly that $\alpha$ must be a bijection. And finally, any right inverse of a bijective function must also be a left inverse. Therefore, $\beta = \alpha^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$\retTwo
\end{myIndent}

\exTwo\ul{Theorem 4.12 (The Rank Theorem):} Suppose $M$ and $N$ are $C^k$ manifolds \textit{without}\\ \textit{boundaries} of dimension $m$ and $n$ respectively, and also suppose $F: M \to N$ is a $C^k$ map of constant rank $r$. Then for each $p \in M$ there exists a smooth chart $(U, \varphi)$ on $M$ with $p \in U$ as well as a smooth chart $(V, \psi)$ on $N$ with $F(U) \subseteq V$ such that $\widehat{F} \coloneqq \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ has the form $\widehat{F}(x_1, \ldots, x_r, x_{r+1}, \ldots, x_m) = (x_1, \ldots, x_r,\hspace{-0.5em} \underbrace{0, \ldots, 0}_{n-r \text{ many zeros}}\hspace{-0.5em})$.\retTwo
\begin{myIndent}\exThreeP
	\begin{myIndent}\myComment
		As a side note, if $k_1 > k_2$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1} \cup \{\infty\}$ then every $C^{k_1}$ structure on a manifold $M$ is contained in a unique $C^{k_2}$ structure. Therefore, it's not too too restrictive to assume $F$ is as smooth a map as the structure on the manifold is smooth. That said, if I didn't make this restriction clear than one might falsely think that this theorem proves every constant rank $C^1$ map between $C^k$ manifolds without boundaries is actually a $C^k$ map.\retTwo
	\end{myIndent}

	Proof:\\
	\ul{(Step 0: Simplying our notation)}\\
	Firstly, by choosing local coordinates around $p$ it suffices to assume $M$ is an open set\\ $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ and $N$ is an open set $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$.\newpage 
	\begin{myIndent}\exPPP
		To see why, consider picking smooth charts $(\widetilde{U}, \widetilde{\varphi})$ on $M$ containing $p$ and $(\widetilde{V}, \widetilde{\psi})$ on $N$ such that $F(\widetilde{U}) \subseteq \widetilde{V}$. Then set $U \coloneqq  \widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{U})$ and $V \coloneqq \widetilde{\psi}(\widetilde{V})$ and note that $U$ and $V$ are open in $\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$ respectively. Once we've shown that the theorem holds for $\widetilde{\psi} \circ F \circ (\widetilde{\varphi})^{-1}: U \to V$, then we can just find two $C^k$ diffeomorphisms\\ [2pt] $\varphi: U_0 \to \varphi(U_0)$ and $\psi: V_0 \to \psi(V_0)$ such that $\widetilde{\varphi}(p) \subseteq U_0 \subseteq U$, $V_0 \subseteq V$, and $\widetilde{\psi} \circ F \circ (\widetilde{\varphi})^{-1}(U_0) \subseteq V_0$, and $(\psi \circ \widetilde{\psi}) \circ F \circ ((\widetilde{\varphi})^{-1} \circ \varphi^{-1})$ has the desired form. And since $(\widetilde{\varphi}^{-1}(U_0), \varphi \circ \widetilde{\varphi})$ is a smooth chart on $M$ and $(\widetilde{\psi}^{-1}(V_0), \psi \circ \widetilde{\psi})$ is a smooth chart on $N$, we'll have shown our theorem holds at $p$ for the map $F$.\retTwo
	\end{myIndent}

	Next, note that since $\Df F(p)$ has rank $r$, we know the Jacobian matrix has some\\ $r \times r$ invertible submatrix. By reordering our coordinates, we can assume without loss\\ of generality that the $r \times r$ invertible submatrix makes up the top left portion of $\Df F(p)$.\\ So, let us rename the standard coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$ as:
	
	{\centering$(x, y) \coloneqq (x_1, \ldots, x_r, y_{r+1}, \ldots, y_m)$ and $(v, w) \coloneqq (v_1, \ldots, v_r, w_{r+1}, \ldots, w_n)$.\retTwo\par}

	By applying a translation to the open sets $U$ and $V$, we can without loss of generality\\ assume $p = (0, 0)$ and $F(p) = (0, 0)$. Also, we write $F(x, y) \coloneqq (Q(x, y), R(x, y))$\\ where $Q: U \to \mathbb{R}^r$ and $R: U \to \mathbb{R}^{n - r}$ are $C^k$ functions. Therefore:

	$$\Df F(0, 0) = \begin{bmatrix} \left[\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial x_j}(0, 0)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq r \\ 1 \leq j \leq r}}\phantom{1} & \left[\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial y_j}(0, 0)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq r \\ r+1 \leq j \leq m}} \\ 
	\left[\frac{\partial R_i}{\partial x_j}(0, 0)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ 1 \leq j \leq r}} & \left[\frac{\partial R_i}{\partial y_j}(0, 0)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ r+1 \leq j \leq m}}\end{bmatrix}$$ 

	where $\left[\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial x_j}(0, 0)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq r \\ 1 \leq j \leq r}}$ is an invertible matrix.\retTwo 

	\ul{(Step 1: Getting $\varphi$)}\\
	Now define $\varphi: U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ by $\varphi(x, y) = (Q(x, y), y)$. This will be $C^k$ map whose total derivative is given by:

	$$\Df \varphi(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} \left[\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial x_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq r \\ 1 \leq j \leq r}} & \left[\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial y_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq r \\ r+1 \leq j \leq m}} \\ \mMat{0} & \mMat{I}_{m - r} \end{bmatrix}$$ 

	where $\mMat{I}_{m - r}$ is the $(m - r) \times (m - r)$ identity matrix. Importantly, we can specifically see that all the columns of $\Df \varphi(0, 0)$ are linearly independent. Hence, $\Df \varphi(0, 0)$ is invertible. And by the inverse function theorem we know there are connected open sets $U_0 \subseteq U$ and $\widehat{U}_0 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ satisfying that $(0, 0) \in U_0$, $\varphi(0, 0) = (0, 0) \in \widehat{U}_0$, and $\varphi: U_0 \to \widehat{U}_0$ is a\\ $C^k$-diffeomorphism. By shrinking $U_0$ and $\widehat{U}_0$ we can without loss of generality assume\\ that $\widehat{U}_0 = (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)^{m}$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$.\retTwo

	Now we write the inverse map $\varphi^{-1}: \widehat{U}_0 \to U_0$ as $\varphi^{-1}(x, y) = (A(x, y), B(x, y))$ where $A: \widehat{U}_0 \to \mathbb{R}^r$ and $B: \widehat{U}_0 \to \mathbb{R}^{m-r}$ are $C^k$ maps. Importantly, we know that:
	
	{\center$(x, y) = \varphi(A(x, y), B(x, y)) = (Q(A(x, y), B(x, y)),\hspace{0.2em} B(x, y))$\retTwo\par}

	By comparing $y$-components we can see that $B(x, y) = y$. Hence, $\varphi^{-1}$ has the form\newpage $\varphi^{-1}(x, y) = (A(x, y),\hspace{0.2em} y)$. Meanwhile, by comparing $x$-components we can see that\\ $x = Q(A(x, y), \hspace{0.2em} y)$. Therefore, upon letting $\widetilde{R}(x, y) \coloneqq R(A(x, y), y)$ then we have\\ that:

	{\centering $(F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y) = (x,\hspace{0.2em} \widetilde{R}(x, y))$ \retTwo\par}

	\ul{(Step 2: Simplifying $\widetilde{R}$)}\\
	Consider that the Jacobian matrix of $F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ at any $(x, y) \in \widehat{U}_0 = (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)^m$ is given by:

	$$\Df (F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} \mMat{I}_r & \mMat{0} \\ \left[\frac{\partial \widetilde{R}_i}{\partial x_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ 1 \leq j \leq r}} & \left[\frac{\partial \widetilde{R}_i}{\partial y_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ r+1 \leq j \leq m}}\end{bmatrix}$$

	\phantom{.}\\
	Yet we also have that $\Df (F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y) = \Df F(\varphi^{-1}(x, y)) \Df (\varphi^{-1})(x, y)$. By assumption, we know that $\mRank(\Df F(\varphi^{-1}(x, y))) = r$ for all $(x, y) \in \widehat{U}_0$. Meanwhile, since $\varphi^{-1}$ is\\ a diffeomorphism, we know that $\Df (\varphi^{-1})(x, y)$ defines a bijective linear map for all\\ $(x, y) \in \widehat{U}_0$. It follows that $\mRank(\Df (F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y))$ will still equal $r$ for all $(x, y) \in \widehat{U}_0$.\retTwo

	Yet the only way this matrix:
	$$\begin{bmatrix} \mMat{I}_r & \mMat{0} \\ \left[\frac{\partial \widetilde{R}_i}{\partial x_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ 1 \leq j \leq r}} & \left[\frac{\partial \widetilde{R}_i}{\partial y_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ r+1 \leq j \leq m}}\end{bmatrix}\textit{,}$$
	can ever have rank $r$ is if $\left[\frac{\partial \widetilde{R}_i}{\partial y_j}(x, y)\right]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq n - r \\ r+1 \leq j \leq m}} = \mMat{0}$.
	\begin{myIndent}\exPPP
		This is because the first $r$ columns are already linearly independent, and the\\ intersection of their span with the span of the last $m - r$ columns is just the\\ trivial subspace $\{0\}$.\retTwo
	\end{myIndent}

	Consequently, we know that $\widetilde{R}$ must be constant with respect to $y$. In other words, there exists some $C^k$ map $S: (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)^r \to \mathbb{R}^{n - r}$ such that $\widetilde{R}(x, y) = S(x)$. And with that, we can write:

	{\centering $(F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y) = (x, S(x))$\retTwo\par}

	\ul{(Step 3: Getting $\psi$)}\\
	Let $V_0 \coloneqq \{(v, w) \in V : (v, 0) \in \widehat{U}_0\}$. Then $V_0$ will be an open subset of $V$ containing $F(0, 0) = (0, 0)$. Also because $\widehat{U}_0$ is a cube and $(F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y) = (x, S(x))$, we can see that $(F \circ \varphi^{-1})(\widehat{U}_0) \subseteq V_0$. (And this also implies $F(U_0) \subseteq V_0$).\retTwo

	To finish off, define $\psi: V_0 \to \mathbb{R}^n$ by $\psi(v, w) = (v, w - S(v))$. Then $\psi$ is a $C^k$ map which is also a diffeomorphism onto its image. After all, by the invariance of domain theorem we know its image is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Also, its inverse map is precisely given by $\psi(v, w) = (v, w + S(v))$.\retTwo

	And now just note that $(\psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1})(x, y) = \psi(x, S(x)) = (x, S(x) - S(x)) = (x, 0)$. $\blacksquare$\newpage
\end{myIndent}

\ul{Theorem 4.14:} Let $M, N$ be $C^k$ manifolds without boundaries and let $F: M \to N$ be a $C^1$ map of constant rank.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item[(a)] If $F$ is surjective then $F$ is a submersion.
	\begin{myIndent}\exThreeP
		Proof:\\
		Let $m = \dim(M)$ and $n = \dim(N)$, and suppose $F$ has constant rank $r$. We'll\\ suppose to the contrary that $F$ is \textit{not} a submersion. Thus $r < n$.\retTwo

		Now without loss of generality we can assume $k = 1$ (i.e. that $M$ and $N$ are $C^1$ manifolds).
		\begin{myIndent}\exPPP
			This is because the $C^k$ structures $\mathcal{A}_M$ and $\mathcal{A}_N$ that $M$ and $N$ are equipped\\ with are both contained in unique $C^1$ structures $\mathcalli{B}_M$ and $\mathcalli{B}_N$ respectively.\\ Furthemore, a map $f: M \to \mathbb{R}$ is $C^1$ with respect to $\mathcal{A}_M$ if and only if\\ it is $C^1$ with respect to $\mathcal{B}_M$. And a similar story holds for any $f: N \to \mathbb{R}$.\\ It follows that the tangents spaces to $M$ and $N$ stay identical when each is\\ equipped with either structure. In turn $F: (M, \mathcal{A}_M) \to (N, \mathcal{A}_N)$ is a\\ submersion if and only if $F: (M, \mathcal{B}_M) \to (N, \mathcal{B}_N)$ is a submersion.\retTwo
		\end{myIndent}

		Due to the prior reasoning, the rank theorem now applies. So, for all $p \in M$ there exists a smooth chart $(U_p, \varphi_p)$ on $M$ containing $p$ and a smooth chart $(V_p, \psi_p)$ on $N$ with $F(U_p) \subseteq V_p$ and $\widehat{F}_p: \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ having the form:
		
		{\centering$\widehat{F}_p(x_1, \ldots, x_r, x_{r+1}, \ldots, x_m) = (x_1, \ldots, x_r, \hspace{-0.75em}\underbrace{0, \ldots, 0}_{\text{nonzero }\#\text{ of zeros}}\hspace{-0.75em})$.\retTwo\par}

		Since $M$ is an LCH space, we can for each $p$ pick a precompact open set $W_p \subseteq M$\\ such that $p \in W_p \subseteq \overline{W_p} \subseteq U_p$. Then as $\overline{W_p}$ is compact and $N$ is Hausdorff, we\\ know that $F(\overline{W_p})$ is a closed subset of $N$ containing $F(W_p)$. Moreover, $F(\overline{W_p})$ has an empty interior. After all, let $\widehat{U}_{p} \coloneqq \varphi_{p}(U_p)$ and $\widehat{V}_{p} \coloneqq \psi_{p}(V_p)$. Then we have that $(\psi \circ F)(\overline{W_p}) \subseteq \widehat{F}_p(\widehat{U}_p) \subseteq (\text{ a hyperplane }) \cap \widehat{V}_p$. Since the latter set has an empty interior relative to $\widehat{V}_p$, we can conclude that $(\psi \circ F)(\overline{W_p})$ has an empty interior\newpage relative to $\widehat{V}_p$ as well. Then since $\psi$ is a homeomorphism from $V_p$ to $\widehat{V}_p$, we can\\ conclude that $F(\overline{W_p})$ has an empty interior relative to $V_p$. And since $F(\overline{W_p})$ is a closed subset of $N$ containing $F(W_p)$ and contained in $V_p$, we can finally conclude that $F(W_p)$ is nowhere dense.\retTwo
		
		But now to get our contradiction, note that because $M$ is second countable there must be a sequence $\{p_\ell\}_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}}$ such that $M = \bigcup_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}} W_{p_\ell}$. Since $F$ is surjective, we in turn know that $N = F(M) = \bigcup_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}}F(W_{p_\ell})$. So, we've proven that $N$ is a countable union of nowhere dense sets. In other words $N$ is meager. Yet $N$ is an LCH space and so this contradicts the Baire category theorem. [See \inLinkRap{placeholder}{pages 540-541}.]\retTwo 
	\end{myIndent}

	\item[(b)] If $F$ is injective then $F$ is an immersion.
	\begin{myIndent}\exThreeP
		Proof:\\
		Let $m$, $n$, and $r$ be as in the prior proof, and this time suppose to the contrary that\\ $F$ is \textit{not} an immersion. For identical reasons as in the last proof, we can assume\\ without loss of generality that $k = 1$ (i.e. that $M$ and $N$ are $C^1$ manifolds). And this is important because it lets us apply the rank theorem.\retTwo 

		Consider any $p \in M$ and let $(U, \varphi)$ and $(V, \psi)$ be smooth charts on $M$ such that $p \in U$, $F(U) \subseteq V$, and $\widehat{F} \coloneqq \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ has the form:

		{\centering$\widehat{F}(x_1, \ldots, x_r, \hspace{-0.75em}\underbrace{x_{r+1}, \ldots, x_m}_{\text{nonzero }\#\text{ of coordinates}}\hspace{-0.5em}) = (x_1, \ldots, x_r, \hspace{-0.5em}\underbrace{0, \ldots, 0}_{n - r \text{ many zeros}}\hspace{-0.5em})$.\retTwo\par}

		But now $F$ being injective implies that $\widehat{F}$ is also injective. Simultaneously, write\\ $\varphi(p) = (p_1, \ldots, p_r, p_{r+1}, \ldots, p_m)$. Since $\varphi(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$ we know for some\\ small enough $\varepsilon > 0$ that $\widehat{q} \coloneqq (p_1, \ldots, p_r, p_{r+1} + \varepsilon, \ldots, p_m) \in \varphi(U)$. But now if we let $q = \varphi^{-1}(q)$ we have that $(\psi \circ F)(p) = \widehat{F}(\varphi(p)) = \widehat{F}(\varphi(q)) = (\psi \circ F)(q)$. Since $\psi$ is injective, we in turn have that $F(p) = F(q)$. Yet $p \neq q$ since $\varphi^{-1}$ is injective. So, this all contradicts that $F$ is injective. $\blacksquare$\retTwo
	\end{myIndent}
\end{enumerate}

\exTwo\ul{Corollary:} Let $M, N$ be $C^r$ manifolds without boundaries and let $F: M \to N$ be a $C^k$ map of constant rank (where $k \leq r$). If $F$ is a bijection then $F$ is a $C^k$ diffeomorphism.
\begin{myIndent}\exThreeP
	Proof:\\
	By the prior theorem, $F$ being a bijection means that $F$ is both a submersion and an\\ immersion. It then follows by proposition 4.8 on \inLinkRap{Proposition 4.8 page 1061}{page 1061} that $F$ is a local $C^k$\\ diffeomorphism. Yet finally, we can easily see by the pasting lemma that any local\\ $C^k$ diffeomorphism that is also bijective is a (global) $C^k$ diffeomorphism. So, $F$ is a $C^k$\\ diffeomorphism. $\blacksquare$\retTwo
\end{myIndent}

Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra (which doesn't have to be commutative). We say a family $\{A_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ of $R$-submodules of $A$ is a \udefine{grading} on $A$ if $A = \bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}A_n$ as an internal direct sum of $R$-modules and $a_r \in A_r$ and $a_s \in A_s$ implies that $a_r a_s \in A_{r + s}$.
\begin{itemize}
	\item An $R$-algebra with a grading $\{A_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is said to be a \udefine{graded $R$-algebra}. If all $A_n = \{0\}$ for $n < 0$ then we say our grading is \udefine{nonnegative}.

\exTwo\ul{Theorem 3.5:} Let $A$ be a unital graded $R$-algebra with the grading $\{A_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$. Then $1_A$ is\\ homogeneous of degree $0$.
\begin{myIndent}\exThreeP
	Let us write $1_A = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} a_n$ as a sum of the homogeneous coefficients of $1_A$. Then for any $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ we consider any $b_m \in A_m$. But now note that:
	
	{\centering$\sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} a_n b_m = 1_A b_m = b_m = b_m1_A = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} b_m a_n$\retTwo\par}

	By comparing homogeneous coefficients, we get that $a_0 b_m = b_m = b_m a_0$ (and also that $a_n b_m = 0 = b_m a_n$ if $n \neq 0$). And thus since every $b \in A$ is a sum of $b_m \in A_m$ as $m$ ranges over some finite subset of $\mathbb{Z}$, we can conclude for all $b \in A$ that $a_0 b = b = b a_0$. This shows that $a_0$ is an identity element of $A$. And thus $1_A = 1_A a_0 = a_0$. $\blacksquare$\retTwo
\end{myIndent}
